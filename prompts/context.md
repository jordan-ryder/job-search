### Better job-finding approach than scrolling LinkedIn

LinkedIn search is genuinely bad. Two improvements:

1. **Boolean searches inside the LinkedIn keyword field.** Example: `"data engineer" NOT senior NOT lead` or `("data engineer" OR "analytics engineer") AND (python OR dbt)`. Save the search, turn on email alerts, scan the digest in 2 minutes a day.
2. **Speed-triage every JD on three things only:** title level, comp (or comp range if shown), remote or not. If all three pass, then read. Cuts review time roughly 4x.

## Target Company List (Inverted Funnel)

Don't search jobs and filter to companies. Search companies and check if they're hiring. Flips the funnel.

**Coarse filters that kill 95%:** remote-friendly, has a real data team (not 200-person consultancy), pays at or above target band ($147K+ senior DE), not in industries that are a pass.

**Realistic universe:** at the intersection of remote, senior DE, modern stack, business-aligned data team, target comp, the national list at any given time is 200-400 companies. Knowable, not infinite.

**Build the list (one evening):** dump anyone passing coarse filter from the seed sources above into a spreadsheet. Should land at 200-300.

**Second pass per company (60s each):** org placement of data team (CDO good, buried in IT bad), recent eng blog or talks (modern stack, honest about tradeoffs), Glassdoor manager-specific reviews (not just overall score). Cuts to ~50.

**Tag the 50:**
- **A tier (10-15):** Apply this week, network aggressively, real energy.
- **B tier (20-25):** Apply when role opens, watch careers page.
- **C tier (10-15):** Only if exactly-right role posts.

A tier is where network outreach concentrates. Any second-degree connection to anyone at an A tier company is a high-priority outreach.

## Network Outreach

Network and direct apply run in parallel. Network paths convert higher and skip steps; direct apply is the wide net. Don't trade one for the other.

### Three modes (keep them separate)

- **Recruiters (Mikaela, etc.):** transactional. They have a role, you're a candidate. Warm and professional, not relationship-building.
- **Old colleagues:** friendly catch-up with soft signal. Reconnect, ask about their current world, let your situation come up naturally.
- **Warm intros from those colleagues:** structured calls with people you've been vouched for. Skip the catching-up, go straight to "here's what I'm exploring, here's why you specifically."

Cold DMs without an intro work occasionally but at much lower yield. Skip when warm intros are available.

### Network inventory (one sitting, ~30 min)

Open LinkedIn, list:
- Former coworkers from prior jobs you actually liked
- Former managers, especially anyone who'd vouch
- People who left Roehl in the last few years (lowest visibility risk; they're already out)
- Vendors, consultants, contractors you worked well with
- Bootcamp / school folks now in tech
- Second-degree connections at companies on the target list

Aim for 30-50 names. Tag A/B/C: A = grab coffee tomorrow, B = friendly DM, C = need a real reason.

### Pacing (sustainable while working full-time)

- 5-7 hours/week, evenings and a weekend block
- 3 outreach messages/week to A/B contacts. Quality over volume.
- 2-4 tailored applications/week
- 1 actual conversation/week (lunch hour, post-bedtime)

8 weeks = ~24 conversations and ~25 applications. Realistic time to signed offer for senior IC at this level: 2-4 months. Not behind.

### Outreach style (introvert version)

Don't ask for a job. Ask for a 20-minute call about their company, their team, what their last search was like if they recently switched. Genuine curiosity converts better than pitch energy. Job leads surface naturally or they don't, and either way it's one real connection. That's how Relator works at its best.

**Mention the search, but late and lightly.** Hiding it is the worse failure mode for an introvert: do the call, never bring it up, and now you've burned social capital for zero search value. Worse, when you reach back out three months later with "actually I'm looking," they'll feel mildly used.

**In the opening message:** soft signal is enough. "Starting to think about what's next, would love to hear how things are going at [company]." They know what it means without you spelling it out, and it gives them an out.

**In the conversation:** spend 80% on them. Their work, team, what's interesting, what's hard. This is the Relator part and it's not performative; you'll find it interesting. When they ask about you (they will), you have an opening:

> "Honestly part of why I reached out is I'm starting to look around. The team I'm on isn't growing the direction I want, so I'm exploring roles with more ownership and a path into leadership. Anything like that on your radar, or anyone you think I should be talking to?"

**Two asks at the end, not one:**
1. Do you know of roles that fit?
2. Who else should I be talking to?

The second one is the multiplier. Most people can't hand you a job, but almost everyone can name two or three other people. That's how 30 conversations becomes 80 without cold-DMing strangers.

### Reframe on what to ask former colleagues

Don't ask what's happening at the company you both used to work at. Nobody tracks their old employers. Ask about **their current company**: how it's going, are they hiring, what's the team like. They know that cold and they like talking about it. Plus the "who else should I talk to" multiplier.

### Reframe on warm intros to strangers

A warm intro is not a cold message. "Sarah suggested I reach out" lands completely differently. Recipient knows Sarah, trusts Sarah, assumes if Sarah said you were worth 20 min, you probably are. They're not deciding whether to talk to a stranger; they're honoring a small favor for a friend. Acceptance rate is high.

The shape:
1. Sarah says "you should talk to Mike at [company]"
2. Ask Sarah if she's willing to introduce. **Don't skip this step and message Mike yourself.**
3. Sarah sends a 3-sentence intro
4. You reply with what you're up to, why Mike specifically, clear small ask (20-min call)
5. Mike says yes most of the time

Feels weird only because it's new. After three of these it's just "introduced person, scheduled call, focused 1:1," which is one of the easier social shapes for a Relator.

## Phone Screen Cliff Notes
- **TMAY:** 45-60 seconds. Current scope, 2-3 concrete projects, what's next. Don't list technologies.
- **Why looking:** "Role isn't growing the direction I want, looking for more ownership and a path into leadership." Forward-looking, no diagnosis.
- **Salary:** have a number ready, target a meaningful step up, easier to negotiate down than up.
- **Counter-offer:** "Not planning on one. I'm looking because the role needs to change, not the comp."
- **Interviewing elsewhere:** "In early conversations with a few places, nothing at offer stage."
- **Disagreements with architects:** sanitized generic answer, do not tell the real story.
- **Start:** two weeks.
- **Tone:** warm, tight, get to round two. Don't win a phone screen, just don't lose it.

## Decision Frame
Don't quit flat. Keep paycheck for negotiating leverage, preserve Jason as a clean reference, keep the interview story forward-looking ("for a better opportunity" not "after a difficult situation"). Use PTO for breathing room. The exit infrastructure is in place; the gap to close is search velocity and a signed offer.

## Communication Patterns to Watch in Interviews
- **Self-deprecation.** Don't narrate weaknesses or shrink yourself ("not as strategic," "more of a listener"). State things neutrally.
- Don't preemptively solve emotions the interviewer hasn't expressed.
- Don't apologize for the schedule constraint or the year of tenure at Roehl.
- Adaptability can mask churn. Know which one you're doing.
